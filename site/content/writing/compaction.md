title: "What Deletion Actually Looks Like"
date: 2026-06-13
excerpt: "How storage engines handle data you thought you erased."
tags: ["engineering"]
---

If you've ever used an AI agent on a long task, you might have noticed it periodically "compacting" its memory, squashing older parts of the conversation into a tight summary so it can keep going without hitting its context limit. It's basically the agent admitting that it can't hold everything in its head at once, so it has to decide what's worth keeping in full and what can be compressed. Databases have been dealing with the exact same problem for decades, just with bytes on disk instead of tokens in a prompt.

The reason compaction exists at all comes down to a fundamental awkward truth about storage: deleting and updating data is surprisingly hard to do cleanly. When you delete a row or overwrite a value, most storage engines don't actually go find that old data and scrub it from disk. That would be painfully slow. Instead, they just write a new entry, either the updated value or a little marker called a tombstone that says "this key is dead now", and leave the old stuff sitting there underneath. Fast to write, but now you've got ghosts accumulating in your storage files.

This is especially visible in LSM tree engines, the architecture behind RocksDB, Cassandra, LevelDB, and a bunch of others. LSM stands for Log-Structured Merge-tree, and the name is pretty literal. Writes land in memory first (a memtable), get flushed to disk as immutable sorted files called SSTables, and then slowly get merged and reorganized through compaction levels as data accumulates. Compaction is the job that actually takes all those overlapping SSTable files, reads through them, throws out the stale versions and tombstones, and rewrites the survivors into clean, consolidated files. Without it, reads would have to dig through dozens of files just to find one value, and your disk would quietly fill up with data you thought you deleted months ago.

The nasty tradeoff here is write amplification. Every byte you write once at the application level might get physically rewritten to disk three, five, ten times as it moves through compaction levels. You wrote one row, the engine wrote it to the memtable, flushed it to an L0 SSTable, merged it into L1, merged it again into L2, and so on. That's not a bug, it's the price of keeping reads fast and storage tidy. The tuning knobs around compaction are basically just ways of deciding who pays the bill, do you want writes to be cheap now and pay later during a heavy compaction run, or do you compact aggressively and spread the cost out more evenly?

B-tree databases aren't off the hook either, they just hide the mess differently. PostgreSQL uses MVCC, multiversion concurrency control, which means every update to a row doesn't overwrite the old one, it writes a brand new version and marks the old one as expired. Great for concurrency, terrible for tidiness. Dead row versions pile up in your tables and indexes, bloating everything. VACUUM is PostgreSQL's answer to this, it crawls through the heap, finds all the dead tuples, and marks their space as reusable. VACUUM FULL goes further and actually rewrites the table from scratch, reclaiming space at the OS level, though it locks the table while it does it which is why most people avoid it unless things have gotten really out of hand.

What's interesting is that compaction is one of those problems where there's no clean solution, just different flavors of compromise. You're always trading between write speed, read speed, storage overhead, and the cost of the compaction work itself. Some workloads are almost entirely writes, telemetry pipelines, event logs, and they benefit from tuning toward less frequent, more aggressive compaction. Others are read-heavy and want compaction running constantly so there's never too much overlap to sift through. The engine can't know which world it's in, so it exposes a pile of tuneable parameters and trusts you to figure it out.

The agent compacting its context window and RocksDB merging its SSTables are doing genuinely different things, but they're both expressions of the same underlying reality: you can't keep everything, doing cleanup is expensive, and the only question is when you pay for it and how.
