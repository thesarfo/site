I recently came across a TikTok where someone talked about the idea of *infinite punishment for a finite crime*. He used Hitler as an example, which is uncomfortable, but also kind of necessary if we’re being honest.

The argument wasn’t that Hitler wasn’t evil. He absolutely was. The point was simpler: even for someone responsible for unimaginable suffering, is there really no point where punishment has been “enough”? There is a level of punishment, however severe, where even in a case like his, you’re forced to admit that nothing further is being achieved. At some point, you look at the suffering and think: this has gone on long enough.

After centuries, thousands of years even, you’d imagine there’s a moment where the punishment no longer serves any purpose. Just endless suffering for the sake of it.

And that’s what got me thinking about [hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hell).

Most traditional ideas of hell suggest eternal punishment. Forever. No end. And when you slow down and really look at that, it starts to feel wildly disproportionate, not just for ordinary people, but for *anyone who has ever lived*. Think about it.

The average human life is maybe 70 or 80 years. Even if someone lived a full century, that’s still an incredibly short window of time to justify infinite punishment. Infinity isn’t just “a really long time.” It’s literally without end. No matter how bad someone was, their crimes happened within a finite, limited span of time.

So how does it make sense to punish them endlessly?

If justice is supposed to be proportional, then eternity breaks the scale completely. Once punishment becomes infinite, degrees of wrongdoing stop mattering. The worst dictator in history and a petty thief end up in the same category: suffering forever. That doesn’t feel like justice.

And this isn’t about excusing evil or minimizing harm. Accountability matters, and actions have consequences. But punishment should *mean something*. It should aim to restore balance, protect others, or at the very least acknowledge proportionality.

Eternal torment does none of that.

It also raises an uncomfortable question about the nature of the being enforcing it. If a system is designed by an all-knowing, all-just, all-merciful entity, why does it resemble the most extreme form of vengeance imaginable? Infinite punishment doesn’t feel like justice.

What makes this even stranger is how casually hell is often discussed. As if eternal suffering is a reasonable response to disbelief, moral failure, or simply being born into the wrong belief system. Billions of people, across history, condemned forever because they were human.

When you really sit with that, it becomes hard to defend.

I’m not claiming to have the perfect answer. Maybe punishment should be finite. Perhaps it should be corrective instead of purely punitive. Some people deserve far more than others, but “forever” feels like a lazy answer to a complex moral problem.

If justice is real, it should make sense. And if it doesn’t, we should be allowed to question it without being accused of defending evil or rejecting morality.
